                Experiment 6: NMOS Source Follower with Current-Mirror

             1. Objective
             To assemble a simple NMOS source follower (common-drain amplifier) biased by an 
             active current-source load built from a current mirror, and to characterize its DC 
             transfer function, small-signal voltage gain, and output resistance — and to verify that a 
             current-source-loaded follower gives near-unity gain and very low output resistance 
             even at low bias current.

             3. Circuit Diagram
             M_ref (diode-connected) and Rref set a reference current Iref ≈ 40 mA. M_ref's gate 
             drives M_cs, mirroring the same current 1:1 to form an active current-source load at the 
             output node. M_sf, the source follower, has its drain tied to VDD and its source tied to 
             this current-source load; the output is taken at the shared M_sf/M_cs node. For the AC 
             measurements (Sections 6.3–6.5), the function generator is coupled onto Vin through a 
             series 1 µF capacitor, with a 1 MΩ R_bias carrying the DC bias from the potentiometer 
             to the gate — the same AC-coupling technique used in Experiment 5, so the DC 
             operating point set in Section 6.2 is undisturbed by the AC source.

         4. Theory
         Because M_cs's gate is fixed (tied to the mirror reference), the current it sinks is fixed at 
         Iref regardless of Vin. Since M_sf and M_cs are the only two current paths into the 
         output node, M_sf must also carry exactly this same fixed current at all times — which 
         means M_sf's own VGS is forced to a single fixed value, independent of Vin. The DC 
         transfer characteristic is therefore simply:
         Vout = Vin − VGS(M_sf), with VGS(M_sf) fixed by Iref  —  an exact unity-slope, level-
         shifted line
         This holds across the whole range where M_sf and M_cs both stay in saturation — for 
         this bias point, from about 1.1 V up to the supply rail, a far wider linear range than the 
         differential amplifier's roughly ±150 mV (Experiment 4). The small-signal voltage gain 
         follows the same reasoning: Av = dVout/dVin = gm/(gm + 1/ro,total) ≈ 1 for high gm and 
         high output resistance of the current-source load; any measured gain below 1 comes 
         from the current source's finite output resistance and body effect, neither of which is 
         characterized on the SI9926BDY datasheet, so this gap is left as something to measure 
         rather than predict precisely.
         The output resistance looking back into Vout is approximately Rout ≈ 1/gm (ignoring 
         body effect and the current source's own output resistance, both of which raise it 
         slightly). Because this device sits in weak inversion at this bias current (as established in 
         Experiment 4), gm = Iref/(nVT) is unusually large for the current level, so Rout comes 
         out remarkably small — on the order of 1 Ω. This is the classic benefit of a source 
         follower: it presents a low-impedance output regardless of how modest the bias current 
         is.
         Frequency response: an exact bandwidth prediction would need the gate and output 
         capacitances under these specific bias and loading conditions, which the datasheet does 
         not characterize (it only gives Ciss/Coss at large power-device currents, not at this 
         weak-inversion bias). Qualitatively, though, two effects point the same way: the very low 
         Rout means the output side is unlikely to show any roll-off within a normal bench sweep, 
         IIT H — EE2301                                       Page 2 of 6
         and the source follower's own gain mechanism does not rely on a high-impedance drain 
         node the way the Experiment 4 amplifier's does. Any roll-off actually observed within the 
         swept range is therefore more likely to come from the input side — the function 
         generator's source resistance and the coupling network interacting with the gate 
         capacitance — than from the follower itself, and is worth identifying rather than 
         assuming away.
         5. Safety Precautions
            •  The SI9926BDY has a low gate threshold (VGS(th) = 0.6–1.5 V, min–max per 
            datasheet) and is ESD-sensitive; ground yourself before handling the breakout 
            boards.
            •  Set the power supply current limit to about 150 mA before connecting the circuit 
            for the first time.
            •  Do not exceed VGS = ±12 V or VDS = 20 V under any circumstance.
            •  Verify all pin connections twice against the datasheet before switching on the 
            supply, and confirm which of the two SI9926BDY packages' channels is used for 
            M_ref, M_cs, and M_sf before wiring.
            •  Always power off before changing any wiring on the breadboard, including when 
            swapping the load resistor for the output-resistance measurement.
         6. Procedure
         6.1 Mirror verification
            •  Assemble the reference branch (Rref, M_ref) and the mirror device M_cs only, with 
            M_sf's gate left open (M_sf not yet connected). Switch on VDD = 12 V with the 
            current limit set to about 150 mA.
            •  Measure the voltage across Rref and compute Iref = VRref/Rref; confirm Iref ≈ 40 
            mA.
         6.2 DC transfer characteristic
            •  Connect M_sf (drain to VDD, source to the M_cs drain node = Vout). Using the 
            potentiometer, sweep Vin from about 1.5 V to 10.5 V in 1 V steps, recording Vout at 
            each step.
            •  Verify the slope (ΔVout/ΔVin) is close to 1 and identify the lowest Vin at which the 
            linear relationship breaks down (M_cs leaving saturation).
         6.3 Small-signal gain
            •  Set Vin's DC bias to about 7 V (mid-range). Add the Cin coupling capacitor and 
            R_bias as shown in Section 3. Apply a small sine signal (start at ~100 mV amplitude, 
            1 kHz) from the function generator and measure the amplitude of Vin and Vout on 
            the oscilloscope. Compute Av = Vout(amplitude)/Vin(amplitude).
         6.4 Frequency response
            •  With the same setup, sweep the function generator frequency from about 10 Hz to 
            100 kHz (e.g., 10, 30, 100, 300, 1 k, 3 k, 10 k, 30 k, 100 k Hz), recording the Vout 
            amplitude at each frequency while keeping Vin's amplitude constant.
         IIT H — EE2301                                          Page 3 of 6
              •  Identify any frequency at which the gain drops noticeably from its mid-band value, 
              and note whether it is closer to the low end or high end of the sweep — this helps 
              distinguish an input-coupling effect from anything else.
           6.5 Output resistance
              •  At 1 kHz, connect each load resistor (10 Ω, 47 Ω, 100 Ω in turn) from Vout to 
              ground, and record the resulting Vout amplitude for each. Power off between 
              changes.
              •  From the loaded-versus-unloaded amplitude ratio at each RL, extract Rout using 
              RL/(RL+Rout) = Vout,loaded/Vout,unloaded, and average the three estimates.
